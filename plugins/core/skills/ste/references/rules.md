# STE writing rules

This is an authored summary of selected ASD-STE100 Issue 9 rules.
It supports routine writing and review, but does not replace the full writing rules or dictionary.
The instructions below can be applied without a separate numbered rule lookup.

## Words and technical terms

Use a general word only with its approved meaning, part of speech and form.
A familiar word can still be unsuitable for a particular use.
For example, `check` and `test` are approved as nouns, but not as general verbs.
Select the alternative that preserves the intended action.

A technical noun names a defined concept in a subject field.
In software documentation, `database`, `field` and `backup file` are examples.
Technical verbs name specialized processes.
Examples include `enter` for user input, `encrypt` for data protection and `reboot` for a system operation.
Use an established project term only with its defined technical meaning and part of speech.
Prefer an approved general verb when it gives the same meaning.

A software term does not gain approval for an unrelated meaning.
For example, `enter` can describe user input, but not movement into a room.
Do not treat a technical noun as a verb, a technical verb as a noun or a new ordinary word as an approved term.
If the available guidance does not establish a term's technical classification or approval, preserve the necessary term.
Identify its approval as unverified in a compliance assessment instead of inventing approval.

Keep one technical term for one concept and explain an unfamiliar term before the reader needs it.
Use American English spelling unless an official requirement specifies another spelling.

Keep ordinary multi-word nouns to no more than three words.
An established technical noun that cannot be divided can be longer.
Write that term in full first, then explain a shorter form or hyphenate words that function as one unit.
Preserve an official name or identifier when its exact form is required.
Do not invent a new noun chain to make a sentence shorter.

## Verbs and pronouns

Use these simple verb forms: infinitive, imperative, simple present, simple past and simple future.
Use a past participle as an adjective, not to build a complex verb construction.
Use an `-ing` form only as a technical noun or as a modifier within a technical noun.
Describe an action with a suitable verb instead of hiding it in an abstract noun.

Use active voice.
In descriptive prose, passive voice is permitted only when the actor is unknown.
Write instructions in the imperative and name the actor when a statement needs one.

Use only approved pronouns and make their reference clear.
The dictionary does not approve `I`, `he` or `she`.
It permits `we` only for the manufacturer, company or organization that releases the documentation.
Use the relevant name, technical role or item when a pronoun is unsuitable or ambiguous.

## Sentences and instructions

Write complete, clear sentences without contractions or omitted words.
Include necessary articles and connecting words.
Rewrite a sentence when direct word substitution changes its meaning or makes it difficult to read.
Avoid unapproved phrasal verbs.

Use no more than 20 words in each procedural sentence.
Give one instruction in each sentence, except when the actions must occur at the same time.
Put a condition first when the reader must know it before doing the action.
Keep procedural notes informative and put required actions in the procedure itself.

Use no more than 25 words in each descriptive sentence.
Give information in a logical order.
Keep each paragraph to one topic and no more than six sentences.
Use consistent key terms so that short sentences still form a connected explanation.

Give a clear safety instruction and explain the risk or possible result.
Use the applicable warning or caution level when a safety instruction is necessary.
Preserve required safety text and do not invent a new risk or reduce an existing one.

## Punctuation and word counts

Use standard English punctuation except semicolons.
Use hyphens where words are directly related, not to hide a long sentence.
Keep required source text and code exact even when their punctuation differs.

For a vertical list:

- Introduce the list with a colon and start each item with a capital letter
- End a complete sentence with a period
- Leave a fragment without a period, except for the last item
- End the last item with a period
- Do not end an item with a comma or semicolon

Count ordinary words separately, with these exceptions that each count as one word:

- A number, including a number written in words
- A number together with its unit of measurement
- An abbreviation, acronym or initialism
- An alphanumeric identifier
- Quoted text, including an exact formula
- A title, heading or exact text on a placard or label
- A proper name of a person, group, organization or geopolitical entity
- A hyphenated group of words

Do not count numbering that only identifies a paragraph or procedure step.
Text in parentheses counts as one word in the containing sentence.
Also count the text inside the parentheses as a separate sentence and apply the sentence-length limit to it.
A colon that introduces a vertical list ends the preceding sentence for the word count.
Count each subsequent list item separately.
Use the 20-word limit for procedural text and the 25-word limit for descriptive text, both before the colon and in each item.
Do not use these conventions to hide unnecessary text in quotes, parentheses or long technical terms.

## Final review

Use words correctly and keep the wording consistent.
Compare the revision with its source for meaning, certainty, requirement strength, actors, numbers and required formats.
Do not replace a possibility with a fact or add a commitment that the source did not make.
Keep a recommendation, requirement, permission and possibility distinct.
Do not replace `should` with `must` when `should` gives a recommendation.
Preserve conditions, exceptions, sequence and time references instead of removing them to shorten a sentence.

When strict simplification cannot preserve a distinction, retain the precise wording.
Identify the departure or unverified point when it materially affects the result.
Do not call that result fully compliant.

An uncertain dictionary meaning requires an available authoritative reference before it can support a compliance claim.
A short sentence, a familiar word or a passed spelling check does not prove STE compliance.
State the limits when an assessment is requested or the missing verification materially affects the result.
